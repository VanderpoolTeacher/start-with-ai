Start With AI

Ninety minutes on handing the parts of your week that eat the most time to an assistant that actually knows your work. Bring a laptop and a real problem you're behind on.

The problem

Three things eat every week

  • PeopleYou meet someone, take their card, and never enter it. Two months later you can't remember the name.
  • TimeThe calendar looks fine until 4:50, when you realize two things are twenty minutes apart and both start at 5:00.
  • InboxFour hundred messages. Six of them are actually waiting on you. Finding those six is the whole job.

The idea

A general assistant gives you generic answers.
An assistant that knows your work gives you your answers.

The difference is not a better model. It's writing down how you actually work — once — in a place the assistant reads every time.

Mechanics

A skill is a folder with a markdown file

~/.claude/skills/
└── morning-plan/
    └── SKILL.md

No install step. No config. No restart. The folder exists, so the skill exists.

Mechanics

Two parts

The one thing people get wrong

The description is the trigger

It isn't documentation for humans. It's what the assistant reads to decide whether this applies to what you just said.

Fires unreliablyFires correctly
"Generates a morning report""Use when the user starts their day or types /morning"
"Email helper""Use when the user asks who is waiting on them or wants a reply drafted"

Name the situations, not the features.

Demo 2 · Calendar

The collisions you can't see in a list

  • PairsOne ends at 3:00 across town, the next starts at 3:00 downtown
  • FamilyA workshop until 5:00 and a pickup at 5:15 — they live in different parts of your head
  • ThreadsYou're meeting her at 2:00 and she asked you something on Monday
  • TravelA 90-minute gap with a 20-minute drive at each end is a 50-minute gap

Demo 3 · Inbox

Four sources, one question

What needs me today?

Something earns a place only if someone is blocked on you, it's due today, or you left it unfinished. Everything else is dropped. A short briefing is a correct briefing.

Demo 3 · The trap

Read your own sent mail first

Your reply often lands in a brand-new thread containing only that one message. The original still looks like it's waiting on you.

A briefing that says "Adam is blocked on you" two hours after you answered him destroys trust in the entire report. Match on recipient and subject — not thread ID, because the thread ID is exactly what differs.

Demo 4 · Email

Two questions per thread

  • FirstIs the last message from someone else? If your reply is last, you handled it.
  • SecondDo they actually need something? "Thanks, got it" is not a request.

Then say what the person wants — "Adam needs a date for the walkthrough" — never "you have an email from Adam."

The boundary

The assistant drafts.
You send.

Not because the drafts are bad. Because a sent email cannot be unsent, and the one time the tone is wrong is the time it goes to the person who matters most.

Build your own

Reads are free. Writes ask. Sends never.

Every skill in the repo is read-only until it has shown you what it plans to do. That's not a limitation — it's why these are usable on a real inbox instead of a test account.

StageWhat it may do
ReadAnything. No permission needed.
WriteOnly after showing the plan and getting a yes. Then re-read it.
SendNever. Full stop.

The habit

When it gets something wrong, add the line

The most valuable lines in a mature skill aren't the steps. They're the warnings — specific, dated, hard-won.

Always pass the IANA timezone name, never a UTC
offset — offsets break recurring events at the
daylight-saving boundary.

A thread only counts as waiting on you if the LAST
message is from someone else.

Six weeks of corrections make a skill worth more than the day you wrote it.

Your turn

Write the skill for the thing you explained twice

Whatever you walked someone through more than once this month — that's almost always the right first skill. Open a folder, write the description as a when, and list the steps.
